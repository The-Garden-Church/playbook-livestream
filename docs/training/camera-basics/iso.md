# ISO and gain

ISO is the setting most people misunderstand. It sounds like it lets the camera "see more light." It doesn't. By the time ISO does anything, the sensor has already collected all the light it's going to get.

ISO turns up the **volume** on that signal, like turning up a microphone. The picture gets brighter, but anything unwanted in the signal gets louder too. In a picture, that unwanted part is **noise**, the grainy speckle you see in dark footage.

## ISO and gain are the same idea

Photo-style cameras usually call it **ISO**. Broadcast and studio cameras often call it **gain**, measured in decibels (dB). They do the same job.

- Doubling the ISO (400 to 800) is one stop brighter.
- Adding 6 dB of gain is also one stop brighter.
- So ISO 800 is about +6 dB from ISO 400, and ISO 1600 is about +12 dB.

## Turn it up last

Because ISO adds noise, it's the last thing you reach for when the picture is too dark. Open the aperture first. If the picture is still dark, then raise the ISO a stop at a time.

Turning ISO down works the other way. Lower ISO is cleaner but darker. Turning it up too far also blows out the bright parts of the picture, like a white shirt under a spotlight. Once something is pure white, there's no detail left to get back.

## Native ISO

Most cameras have one or two **native ISO** settings where the sensor looks its cleanest. Staying at or near a native ISO is a good habit. Your camera's manual lists them.

??? note "On Blackmagic cameras"
    Tap the ISO value on the touchscreen to change it. Studio cameras show gain in dB in some menus and ISO in others. Many Blackmagic cameras have two native ISOs, a lower one for bright rooms and a higher one for dark rooms. Check the manual for your model.

## What to remember

- ISO doesn't add light. It amplifies the light the sensor already caught.
- More ISO means more noise, so use it last.
- Double the ISO, or add 6 dB, for one stop brighter.
